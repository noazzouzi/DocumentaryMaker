# Drama / commentary — writer's guide

## Goals

1. Keep a viewer for 15–30 minutes with a story that escalates: every chapter makes the situation worse, stranger or more expensive than the last.
2. Be fair and accurate while being entertaining: every claim matches its status as of the research date, every quote is verbatim, every number comes from the fact sheet.
3. Give the edit something to cut on: short visual ideas, concrete nouns, names, dates and numbers the viewer can see.
4. Earn the runtime with commentary: the narrator explains, connects and reacts; clips and documents are evidence, not filler.

## Rules

1. **Hook in 5 s.** The first line and the first shot pay off the title and thumbnail promise within 5 seconds. The cold open (≤ 60 s) shows rock bottom, contrasts it with the peak, fires 3–6 escalating teasers and ends on a one-sentence promise. No greeting, no channel intro, no "in this video".
2. **BUT / THEREFORE, never AND THEN.** Consecutive beats are linked by causality or reversal. If "and then" fits between two sentences, rewrite one of them.
3. **A device at least every 120 s** (aim for every 60–90 s): open loop, re-hook, pattern interrupt, callback, cliffhanger, punchline, rhetorical question, reveal or payoff. Tag the segment that performs it.
4. **Commentary after every clip.** Set the clip up in the preceding line, keep it short (the line that matters), then react in one short opinionated sentence. Never two clips back to back.
5. **One read at a time.** Never make the viewer read a card while the narrator says something different. Quotes and documents shown on screen are read by the narrator or heard in the clip.
6. **Selective on-screen text.** Only names, numbers, dates and the one or two words that matter (emphasis words). At most one keyword slam per minute. Never put the narration on screen.
7. **No channel names.** Never name, imitate or reference other channels or creators as style references, and never use their logos or catchphrases.
8. **Status-true wording.** Allegations are attributed and paired with the response ("according to…, which he has always denied"); charged ≠ convicted; settlements are "without admission" when they are; each ruling carries its court, jurisdiction and year. Opinion is signposted ("to me", "it's hard not to see…") and rests on facts already stated. No diagnoses of anyone.
9. **Specific and sourced.** Prefer "$8 billion" to "a lot of money", "194 players" to "many". Every on-screen quote, headline and number references the fact sheet (quote_id, source_id, figure_id).
10. **Spoken rhythm.** Sentences average 11–18 words with regular 2–5-word punches. Write numbers, dates and currencies as a narrator says them.
11. **Structure.** Follow the act shares of the chosen story shape; put the false redemption at 60–70 %; place ad breaks right after a cliffhanger, before its payoff.
12. **Full-circle ending.** Return to the cold open, give the current status as of the research date, state the thesis, then bridge to the next story (the "rabbit hole"). No "thanks for watching", no begging for likes.
13. **Protect people who did not choose the spotlight.** Never name or show minors, private individuals or alleged victims of sexual offences without explicit approval; never describe self-harm methods.

## Reads timing

- Narration runs at about 16.5 characters per second in English and 16 in French; budget in characters, not words.
- One beat = one visual idea = an exact slice of the narration: 1–3 s in the hook, 1.5–6.5 s in the body, about 3.2 s on average.
- On-screen text must stay readable for at least max(1.5 s, characters ÷ 15 + 1.5 s) after its entrance; a title card at least 4 s; a lower third 3–5 s; a caption group at least max(1.8 s, speech + 0.6 s).
- Speed comes from fewer words per screen, never from shorter holds. A 12-word headline needs ~2.3 s of narration under it — write the line so it lasts that long.
- Chapter cards need the narrator to pause: about one second plus a second per 20 characters of title.

## Common failures

- A cold open that explains instead of teasing, or that takes 90 s to reach the first shock.
- Chains of "then… then… then…" chronology with no reversal.
- A clip dropped in without setup, or followed straight by the next fact.
- Beats that are too long (one visual for 12 s) or too abstract to find footage for ("the situation got worse").
- Accusations in the narrator's own voice, or "allegedly" used as a shield.
- Rounded or invented numbers; a quote paraphrased inside quotation marks.
- On-screen text that repeats the narration word for word.
- A tidy moral at the end instead of the current status and an open question.

## Worked example

Fictional company, for illustration only.

**Cold open (rock bottom → peak → teasers → promise):**

> *[HOOK, archival photo of an empty office]* Two years ago, Quillon was worth four billion dollars. *(punchline)* Today, its headquarters is for rent — and its founder can't leave the country. *[REVEAL → stamp CLOSED]*
> *[TIME_JUMP 2019]* It started with a grocery app that promised delivery in nine minutes. *(open_loop)*
> But the nine minutes had a price nobody saw. *[TENSION_BUILD]*
> A warehouse that didn't exist. A loan signed twice. And an email that was never supposed to leave the building. *[LIST → evidence board]*
> This is how a company sold speed — and ran out of time. *(promise)*

**Beat slicing (body, ~3 s each):** "In March 2021, / Quillon raised another 600 million dollars / [NUMBER 600000000 → money counter, figure_id N4] / — therefore the board stopped asking questions." Each slice gets one concrete visual: the funding announcement (headline_stack with its source_id), a counter, the boardroom photo.

**Clip + commentary:** *(setup)* "When a reporter asked about the missing warehouse, the founder had an answer ready." → *[clip: the verbatim answer, quote_id Q7]* → *(reaction)* "An answer, yes. Just not to the question."

**Status-true wording:** not "He stole the money" but "Prosecutors allege he moved 40 million dollars to a private account; he has pleaded not guilty, and the trial is set for next spring."

## Banned

- Generic intros and outros: "in this video", "today we're going to", "before we start", "without further ado", "let's dive in", "smash that like button", "don't forget to subscribe", "thanks for watching" (FR: « dans cette vidéo », « aujourd'hui on va », « sans plus attendre », « abonnez-vous »).
- "Here's the thing" and other filler pivots.
- Narrator-as-fact accusations: stating that a person committed a crime or abuse that is only alleged, settled or dismissed.
- Invented tweets, posts, comments, headlines, document lines, quotes or numbers — anything shown on screen must reference the fact sheet.
- AI-generated images of real people, real logos or real documents; photorealistic AI depictions of real events.
- Naming or showing minors, private individuals or alleged victims of sexual offences; speculating about anyone's health.
- Channel names, creator names or their logos as references; copying another creator's catchphrases or thumbnails.
