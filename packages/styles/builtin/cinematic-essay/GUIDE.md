# Cinematic essay — writer's guide

## Goals

1. Hold a viewer for 20–30 minutes with curiosity rather than shock: each chapter answers one question and opens a better one.
2. Argue honestly: separate what the sources show from interpretation, and say where the record runs out.
3. Write images: give the edit wide, concrete, period-specific visuals — places, objects, documents, faces of the time.
4. Leave room: the film needs pauses, silences and held images; not every second is narrated.

## Rules

1. **Hook in 5 s.** Open on one striking image or detail and a question the film will answer; the cold open (≤ 90 s) ends on the thesis question. No greeting, no channel intro.
2. **BUT / THEREFORE.** Even a slow essay moves by causality and reversal, never by "and then".
3. **A device at least every 150 s** — open loop, callback, rhetorical question, reveal, payoff — quieter than in drama, but present.
4. **Commentary after every clip:** reflect on what was just heard; never two clips back to back.
5. **One read at a time.** A document on screen is read aloud or left in silence; never narrate something else over it.
6. **Selective on-screen text:** names, places, dates and sources only; no slams, no stamps.
7. **No channel names.** Never cite other channels or creators as references; describe, don't name.
8. **Status-true wording** for anything legal or disputed; mark interpretation as interpretation ("one reading is…", "historians disagree…"). Theories stay theories.
9. **Specific and sourced:** exact dates and numbers from the fact sheet, with their source when shown.
10. **Rhythm:** sentences average 12–22 words, with short lines to land a thought; numbers written as spoken.
11. **Breathing room:** mark music breaths after big ideas; a chapter may end on an image without words.
12. **Ending:** return to the opening image, give the answer the evidence supports, name what remains unknown, then bridge to a related story. No "thanks for watching".

## Reads timing

- Narration runs at about 15 characters per second in English and 14.5 in French.
- One beat = one visual idea: 2–4 s in the hook, 3–8 s in the body (about 4.6 s on average).
- On-screen text stays at least max(1.5 s, characters ÷ 15 + 1.5 s); chapter cards at least 4 s, with a pause in the narration (about one second plus a second per 20 characters).
- Dissolves take 0.5–1 s: do not cut a thought across a dissolve.

## Common failures

- An opening that summarises the whole film instead of posing one question.
- Lecture mode: long chains of facts without a turn or an image.
- Vague visuals ("the economy at the time") that cannot be sourced.
- Dramatic music and punchy wording that fight the style.
- Presenting a contested interpretation as settled.

## Worked example

Fictional subject, for illustration only.

> *[HOOK, archival photo: an empty harbour at dawn]* In the spring of 1891, the harbour of Ostrava Bay was the busiest in the region. *(open_loop)* Ten years later, it did not appear on a single map.
> *[TIME_JUMP 1891 → map_route]* To understand why, we have to start with a ship that never arrived. *(promise)*

Body slice: "The harbour master's ledger for that year / survives in a provincial archive. / [DOCUMENT → document_highlight, source_id S4, quote_id Q2] / Its last entry is a single line." — then a music breath over the ledger, then the line read aloud.

Interpretation, signposted: "One reading is that the town simply moved inland. The ledger suggests something less tidy."

## Banned

- Generic intros and outros ("in this video", "let's dive in", "don't forget to subscribe", « abonnez-vous »).
- "Here's the thing" and other filler pivots.
- Narrator-as-fact accusations, and theories stated as facts.
- Invented quotes, documents, headlines, posts or numbers; anything on screen references the fact sheet.
- AI-generated images of real people, real documents or real events.
- Naming or showing minors, private individuals or alleged victims without approval.
- Channel names, creator names or logos as references.
